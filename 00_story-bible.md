## 1. The book in one paragraph

Milo is the loudest, happiest, most fun kid on the playground, but a "NO!" jumps out of him every time someone asks a question or tries to change a game. One day, after he yells his friend off the basketball court, Milo notices something strange: a little bar of five squares floating over his friend's head, and two of the squares have just gone dark. Milo starts testing what makes the squares fill and empty, even on the family cat. His big brother's favorite book gives him an idea: maybe a change isn't the story breaking. Maybe it's a **plot twist**. He tries it, fumbles it, and discovers he has a meter of his own. By the end he catches the NO before it escapes, turns a song interruption into a duet, and learns that someone else might be able to see his meter too.

## 2. Purpose and design principles (written for Alex, never shown to him)

| # | Principle | How the book honors it |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | **Not about him.** Alex dislikes being copied or told what to do. | Milo is "like him" (same energy, hairstyle, expression), not "him" (different name, hair color, family details). No direct callbacks to Alex's real life beyond the general shapes of the triggers. |
| P2 | **No lectures.** Oppositional kids resist the moral when an adult delivers it. | No parent or teacher ever states the lesson. Milo **discovers** the meters, **tests** them, and **invents** his own strategy. The "plot twist" idea is overheard from a book, not instructed. |
| P3 | **The NO is a reflex, not who he is.** | The NO is personified: it "jumps out before Milo can catch it." This separates the behavior from the boy (*externalizing*, a narrative-therapy technique). The arc becomes "Milo learns to catch the NO," not "Milo stops being bad." |
| P4 | **High IQ = respect his intelligence.** | The meter has consistent, discoverable rules (§5), like game mechanics. Milo runs experiments, including on Boris. There are hidden details to reward re-reading. |
| P5 | **High emotion = validate big feelings.** | Milo's frustration is drawn honestly and never mocked. The book shows the *feeling* rising and a *pause* happening, not the feeling disappearing. |
| P6 | **Repair is always possible.** | Empty squares can always be refilled. Nothing is permanently broken. Squares "come back slower than they go." |
| P7 | **He wants to engage.** | Every NO is immediately followed by a sign that Milo actually wanted to say yes. The reader always knows Milo is a good friend underneath. |
| P8 | **Compassion is universal.** | Everyone has a meter, animals included. Boris's meter works by the same rules: needs met, needs ignored, and even "too much of a good thing." |

### 3.2 Milo (protagonist, 7, 2nd grade)
- **Personality:** boisterous, joyful, social, inventive (he makes up games), very loud. He has a strong sense of how a game *should* go.
- **The flaw:** the reflex NO. He also shouts over people when they try to adjust things.
- **What he wants:** to play, to be the game-maker, to have friends around him.
- **What he needs:** to see that other people's ideas don't break his games; they can make them better. Yelling empties everyone's meter, including his own.
- **Visual (locked):**
  - **Hair:** blond, "messy curtain" style: middle part with long, wavy locks falling to either side.
  - **Face:** very expressive. A big open-mouth grin when happy; furrowed brows and scrunched fists when the NO comes out.
  - **Outfit (every page):** plain light-blue T-shirt, blue jeans, blue sneakers.

### 3.3 Diego (friend, 7)
- Hispanic boy, Milo's age. Loves basketball; usually carries or spins one.
- Easygoing and creative. He likes adding twists to games (trick shots, new rules).
- Being yelled at doesn't make him angry; it makes him **quiet**, and he walks away. (Shows kids that hurt often looks like quiet, not anger.)
- **Visual:** basketball jersey or tank top, sneakers, short dark hair, ball always nearby.

### 3.4 Nia (friend, 7)
- Black girl, Milo's age, noticeably **taller** than Milo.
- Loves pretty things: flowers, sparkly hair clips, making things fancy. She turns ordinary games into elaborate ones (the swings become a "sky kingdom").
- Confident, kind, and **observant**, which matters for the ending.
- **Visual:** **always smiling** (her default expression; on her two hurt beats the smile falters, it never turns to tears). Long, feminine hair (not an afro style) with a signature flower clip on every page. Bright dress or skirt with leggings; sometimes holds a flower.

### 3.5 Owen (older brother, 10)
- Three years older. Genuinely enjoys playing with Milo. Loves books; often seen reading on the couch.
- He's the source of the "plot twist" idea, but only by **reading aloud to himself** and exclaiming "PLOT TWIST!" He never advises Milo.
- **Visual (locked):**
  - **Hair:** blond (like Milo), traditional cut with a side part and long bangs. No glasses.
  - **Outfit (every page):** muted green T-shirt, brown shorts, white socks.
  - **Props:** always a book (plain cover, no real titles), and **always a black-and-white cat lying next to him**. The cat is unnamed, a calm accessory that softens him, and never part of the plot.

### 3.6 Boris (family cat, Russian Blue)
- Silver-blue coat, green eyes. The family's cat; **likes to play with Milo** (chases his shoelaces, pounces on piano keys, follows him around).
- The test subject for Milo's cat-meter experiments in Act 2 (§5.2), and a comic witness in later home scenes.

### 3.7 Mom and Dad
- Warm and present but **peripheral**. They ask the ordinary questions that trigger Milo's NOs ("Pancakes?" "Bath time!"). They never deliver the lesson. Their meters appear during Milo's experiments.

## 4. Settings (only two)

### 4.1 Home
- **Living room:** an upright piano against a wall with a window beside it, a couch (Owen's reading spot, with his cat), a bookshelf, and a sunny rug (Boris's spot).
- **Kitchen:** for the opening "No!" gags and Boris's food bowl.
- **Season:** autumn. Warm afternoon light; red, orange, and yellow leaves visible through the window.

### 4.2 Playground (park)
- A half-court basketball hoop on blacktop, a swing set, one big tree, grass. Optional slide.
- Autumn: red/orange/yellow watercolor leaves on the tree and scattered on the ground. A puddle near the swings (needed in Act 3).

## 5. The Happy Meter: rules (canon)

These rules are **never stated as a list in the book**. Milo discovers them one page at a time, and readers can work them out alongside him.

### 5.1 People
1. **Everyone has one.** It's a horizontal bar of **5 squares** floating above the head. **Baseline is 4 of 5.**
2. **What empties it:** being yelled at or shouted over, having your idea answered with "NO!", being ignored, being left out. A normal hurt costs 1 square; a big yell can cost 2.
3. **What fills it:** being listened to ("What's your idea?"), having your idea tried, laughing together, **being complimented**, **succeeding at something**, **being hugged**, and a real apology followed by a real fix.
4. **Squares come back slower than they go.** (This is a refrain line.) Filling happens one square at a time. Emptying can be fast.
5. **Only Milo can see them, at first.** The reader sees a meter **only when Milo is looking at that person**. Before the discovery page, no meters are drawn anywhere.
6. **Milo has his own** (revealed in Act 3). Yelling empties **his** meter too. This is the book's deepest insight: the NO doesn't make *him* happy either.
7. **Someone else might see Milo's meter** (the ending). It stays a **mystery**; the book never confirms who can see what.
8. **Milo only sees his own meter in reflections** (a puddle, the glossy piano, a mirror). The reader sees Milo's meter only in those reflection shots, which keeps the "you can't see your own without looking" logic consistent.
9. **Final page:** time has passed and Milo is back at baseline (4). His wry "plot twist" response to Bath time **refills one square** (4 → 5). No square is ever added; every meter has a fixed size.

### 5.2 Animals ("compassion is universal")
- **Boris has a 2-square meter**, and it follows the same logic as people's:
  - **Meowing and not being fed:** the meter drains **slowly** (not a sudden drop; the need just grows).
  - **Being fed:** refills.
  - **Too much petting:** **overfills** the bar (a third square bulges or pops out past the end), which triggers a **reaction**: Boris swats, flattens his ears, or bolts. Then the bar settles back to normal.
- The lesson Milo (and the reader) discovers: **paying attention** to what someone needs matters, and even good things can be too much if you don't notice when someone has had enough.
- **Owen's black-and-white cat** has a 2-square meter that is **always full**, because it's always sleeping contentedly next to Owen. (A quiet visual joke, and a living example of "needs met = full.")

### 5.3 Meter visual spec (applied in Canva, never generated by AI)
- **Width = one head width** of the character it belongs to, so it's clear and readable at a glance. Centered just above the head.
- 5 rounded squares in a row (2 for Boris and Owen's cat), with slight hand-drawn wobble to match the watercolor.
- **Full square:** warm golden-yellow watercolor fill **with a clear ink outline**. **Empty square:** ink outline only, no fill.
- **A square that just emptied:** small "puff" marks beside it, like a cartoon motion line.
- **A square that just filled:** tiny sparkle marks.
- **Overfill (Boris):** an extra square bulging past the end of the bar, with wobble/vibration lines.
- **Contrast with autumn leaves:** the meter's yellow is more saturated and outlined in ink; leaves are soft, outline-free washes. **Keep leaves out of the meter zone** above heads so the two never compete.

## 6. Story arc (high level; page-level detail goes in the beat sheet)

Target: **28 interior pages** (+ cover, dedication).

### Act 1: Meet Milo and his NO (pp. 1–3)
- Milo is pure fun: inventing games, running, laughing loudly.
- The NO gag: Mom asks "Pancakes?" and Milo shouts "NO!", then quietly, "...yes." The NO jumps out before he can catch it. *(Refrain established.)*

### Act 2: Change 1, **Struggle**: the basketball game (pp. 4–12)
- **Playground, HORSE.** It's Milo's game, played Milo's way. Diego grins: "New rule: every shot has to be a trick shot!"
- The NO jumps out. Milo yells over Diego until Diego stops talking.
- Diego goes quiet, picks up his ball, and walks away.
- **Discovery page:** as Diego turns, Milo sees five squares above his head, and **two of them go dark**. Milo rubs his eyes. *(First meter in the book.)*
- **Home that evening:** Owen is reading on the couch, his cat curled beside him, and shouts "PLOT TWIST!" Milo asks what that is. Owen, still reading: "When the story suddenly goes a different way. It's the best part. You never know what happens next." Milo says nothing, but he files it away.
- **The experiments** (the high-IQ pages): Milo now sees meters on Mom, Dad, and Owen. He tests: "Thanks, Dad" fills a square. Hugging Mom fills a square. Grumbling at Owen empties one.
- **The Boris experiments** (comic relief): Boris meows at his empty bowl and his little 2-square meter drains. Milo feeds him and it refills. Milo pets him... and pets him... and pets him, and the meter overfills, **SWAT!** Boris bolts. Milo, holding his hand, figures out a new rule.

### Act 3: Change 2, **Stumble**: the swings (pp. 13–19)
- **Playground.** Milo wants tag. Nia wants to turn the swings into a "sky kingdom."
- The NO jumps out. Nia's meter drops and her smile falters. **This time Milo sees it happen**, and he freezes.
- He tries to fix it, badly: "FINE. We'll play your dumb kingdom." *(Another square drops; the stumble.)*
- He tries again, quieter: "...What's your idea?" Nia explains, a square returns, and they swing. It's okay, not great. *Squares come back slower than they go.*
- **His own meter:** he hops off the swing and looks into the puddle. Above his own reflection is a meter, and it's low too. Yelling emptied *his*.

### Act 4: Change 3, **Success**: the piano (pp. 20–28)
- **Home.** Diego and Nia come over. Milo plays his song on the piano, his way.
- Nia slides onto the bench and starts playing along, right over him.
- **The NO rises** (drawn as a word bubble pushing up from his chest). Milo squeezes his eyes shut, holds it, and thinks: *Plot twist?* *(Refrain payoff: "This time, Milo caught it.")*
- "PLOT TWIST! It's a duet now!" Diego drums on a box. Owen looks up from his book. Boris pounces onto the keys.
- **All meters fill**, square by square, including Milo's.
- **Mystery beat:** Nia leans over: "Your meter's full, you know." Milo: "You can SEE it?!" Nia just smiles. No answer is ever given.
- **Last page:** another everyday change arrives: Mom calls "Bath time!", an echo of the Act 1 pancakes gag. In the bathroom mirror, Milo gives a **wry smile**: "...Plot twist." **One square refills** on his meter (4 → 5).

### Refrains (the repeated lines that carry the theme)
1. *"The NO jumped out before Milo could catch it."* (Acts 1–3) → *"This time, Milo caught it."* (Act 4)
2. *"Squares come back slower than they go."* (Acts 2–3)
3. *"Plot twist!"* (Owen in Act 2 → Milo in Act 4 → Milo's wry version on the last page)

## 7. Visual style guide

### 7.1 Style description (the "style anchor"; never name a real artist in prompts)
> Loose, confident ink linework with varied line weight; delicate transparent watercolor washes; a mostly white background with only suggested scenery (a hoop, a swing, a piano edge); a soft, muted, warm autumn palette; expressive, exaggerated cartoon faces; dynamic kid poses; a hand-drawn newspaper-comic feel; lots of negative space; calm and clear.

### 7.2 Page composition rules
- **8×8 in square pages**, one frame per page, a single focal action.
- **Nothing but art and words.** No borders, no panel frames, no page numbers, no decorative elements.
- **Text placement:** complementary to the artwork, **generally in the upper half on the left or right**, sitting in the white space beside the figures, the way classic newspaper comic panels place narration. Art must be generated with that corner left open.
- **Meter zone:** clear white space above characters' heads, one head width wide.
- Characters stay at a consistent scale relative to each other: Nia is taller than Milo and Diego; Owen is the tallest kid.
- Backgrounds stay sparse: 1–3 setting props per page. White wins.
- Emotional peaks (the discovery, the puddle, the duet) get **more white space**, not more detail.

### 7.3 Palette
- **Milo:** blond hair; light-blue tee, blue jeans, blue sneakers. (All-blue outfit gives him a single, instantly findable color identity.)
- **Diego:** orange/teal (basketball tones).
- **Nia:** pinks, lavender, a gold accent on her flower clip.
- **Owen:** blond hair; muted green tee, brown shorts, white socks; black-and-white cat.
- **Boris:** blue-grey with green eyes.
- **Settings:** autumn reds, oranges, and yellows as soft washes; neutral warm whites.
- **Meter:** saturated golden yellow with an ink outline (§5.3). It's the boldest color on any page.

### 7.4 Lettering
- A hand-lettered comic-style font for dialogue, and a clean, readable font for narration. Final choice is made in Canva.
- "NO!" and "PLOT TWIST!" get special display lettering. The NO can be drawn as a jagged bubble that "jumps out."
