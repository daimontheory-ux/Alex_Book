## 1. The book in one paragraph

Milo is the loudest, happiest, most fun kid on the playground, but a "NO!" jumps out of him every time someone asks a question or tries to change a game. One day, after he yells his friend off the basketball court, Milo notices something strange: a little bar of five squares floating over his friend's head, and two of the squares have just gone dark. Milo starts testing what makes the squares fill and empty. His big brother's favorite book gives him an idea: maybe a change isn't the story breaking. Maybe it's a **plot twist**. He tries it, fumbles it, and discovers he has a meter of his own. By the end he catches the NO before it escapes, turns a song interruption into a duet, and learns that someone else has been able to see his meter all along.

## 2. Purpose and design principles (written for Alex, never shown to him)

| # | Principle | How the book honors it |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | **Not about him.** Alex dislikes being copied or told what to do. | Milo is "like him" (same energy, hairstyle, expression), not "him" (different name, hair color, family details). No direct callbacks to Alex's real life beyond the general shapes of the triggers. |
| P2 | **No lectures.** Oppositional kids resist the moral when an adult delivers it. | No parent or teacher ever states the lesson. Milo **discovers** the meters, **tests** them, and **invents** his own strategy. The "plot twist" idea is overheard from a book, not instructed. |
| P3 | **The NO is a reflex, not who he is.** | The NO is personified: it "jumps out before Milo can catch it." This separates the behavior from the boy (a narrative-therapy technique called *externalizing*). The arc becomes "Milo learns to catch the NO," not "Milo stops being bad." |
| P4 | **High IQ = respect his intelligence.** | The meter has consistent, discoverable rules (§5), like game mechanics. Milo runs experiments. There are hidden details to reward re-reading (the cat's meter, §5.6). |
| P5 | **High emotion = validate big feelings.** | Milo's frustration is drawn honestly and never mocked. The book shows the *feeling* rising and a *pause* happening, not the feeling disappearing. |
| P6 | **Repair is always possible.** | Empty squares can always be refilled. Nothing is permanently broken. Squares "come back slower than they go," which is realistic without being hopeless. |
| P7 | **He wants to engage.** | Every NO is immediately followed by a sign that Milo actually wanted to say yes (he follows the friend, he looks longingly, he says "...yes" quietly). The reader always knows Milo is a good friend underneath. |

### 3.2 Milo (protagonist, 7, 2nd grade)
- **Personality:** boisterous, joyful, social, inventive (he makes up games), very loud. He has a strong sense of how a game *should* go.
- **The flaw:** the reflex NO. He also shouts over people when they try to adjust things.
- **What he wants:** to play, to be the game-maker, to have friends around him.
- **What he needs:** to see that other people's ideas don't break his games; they can make them better. Yelling empties everyone's meter, including his own.
- **Signature visual:** **[OPEN]** Same hairstyle as Alex (describe: ______). Hair color **different** from Alex's (proposal: ______). Same expressive face: big open-mouth grin when happy, furrowed brows and scrunched-up fists when the NO comes out. Simple recurring outfit (proposal: striped T-shirt + shorts + sneakers), the same on every page.

### 3.3 Mateo (friend, 7)
- Hispanic boy, Milo's age. Loves basketball; usually carries or spins one.
- Easygoing and creative. He likes adding twists to games (trick shots, new rules).
- Being yelled at doesn't make him angry; it makes him **quiet**, and he walks away. (Shows kids that hurt often looks like quiet, not anger.)
- **Visual:** basketball jersey or tank top, sneakers, short dark hair, ball always nearby.

### 3.4 Nia (friend, 7)
- Black girl, Milo's age, noticeably **taller** than Milo.
- Loves pretty things: flowers, sparkly hair clips, making things fancy. She turns ordinary games into elaborate ones (the swings become a "sky kingdom").
- Confident and kind. She's **observant**, which matters for the ending (§6, Act 4).
- **Visual:** bright dress or skirt with leggings, a signature hair accessory (e.g., a flower clip she wears on every page), sometimes holds a flower.

### 3.5 Henry (older brother, 10)
- Three years older. Genuinely enjoys playing with Milo. Loves books; often seen reading on the couch.
- He's the source of the "plot twist" idea, but only by **reading aloud to himself** and exclaiming "PLOT TWIST!" He never advises Milo.
- **Visual:** taller, glasses optional **[OPEN]**, always has a book (one stand-in book with a plain cover, no real titles).

### 3.6 Boris (cat, Russian Blue)
- Silver-blue coat, green eyes, calm and unbothered. Appears on most home pages as a quiet comic witness (on the piano, on Henry's lap, in a sunbeam).
- **Owner:** Henry's cat? the family's cat? **[OPEN]**

### 3.7 Mom and Dad
- Warm and present but **peripheral**. They ask the ordinary questions that trigger Milo's NOs ("Pancakes?" "Time for a bath?"). They never deliver the lesson. Their meters exist and appear during Milo's experiments (§6, Act 2).

## 5. The Happy Meter: rules (canon)

These rules are **never stated as a list in the book**. Milo discovers them one page at a time, and readers can work them out alongside him.

1. **Everyone has one.** It's a horizontal bar of **5 squares** floating just above the head. Most people walk around at 4 of 5.
2. **What empties it:** being yelled at or shouted over, having your idea answered with "NO!", being ignored, being left out. A normal hurt costs 1 square; a big yell can cost 2.
3. **What fills it:** being listened to ("What's your idea?"), having your idea tried, laughing together, a real apology followed by a real fix.
4. **Squares come back slower than they go.** (This is a refrain line.) Filling happens one square at a time. Emptying can be fast.
5. **Only Milo can see them, at first.** The reader sees a meter **only when Milo is looking at that person**. Before the discovery page, no meters are drawn anywhere.
6. **Easter egg: Boris has 9 squares** (nine lives), and they are always full. The book never explains this.
7. **Milo has his own** (revealed in Act 3). Yelling empties **his** meter too. This is the book's deepest insight: the NO doesn't make *him* happy either.
8. **Someone else can see Milo's meter** (revealed on the final pages). Whether everyone can see meters is left a mystery.

### 5.1 Meter visual spec (applied in Canva, never generated by AI)
- 5 rounded squares in a row, with slight hand-drawn wobble to match the watercolor.
- **Full square:** warm golden-yellow watercolor fill. **Empty square:** thin ink outline only, no fill. (Yellow vs. empty avoids relying on red/green.)
- **A square that just emptied:** small "puff" marks beside it, like a cartoon motion line.
- **A square that just filled:** tiny sparkle marks.
- Meter is about **¼ of a head width** above the head, centered. The art must leave clear white space there (see §7).

## 6. Story arc (high level; page-level detail goes in the beat sheet)

Target: **28 interior pages** (+ cover, dedication). Page budgets are approximate.

### Act 1: Meet Milo and his NO (pp. 1–3)
- Milo is pure fun: inventing games, running, laughing loudly.
- The NO gag: Mom asks "Pancakes?" and Milo shouts "NO!", then quietly, "...yes." The NO jumps out before he can catch it. *(Refrain established.)*

### Act 2: Change 1, **Struggle**: the basketball game (pp. 4–12)
- **Playground, HORSE.** It's Milo's game, played Milo's way. Mateo grins: "New rule: every shot has to be a trick shot!"
- The NO jumps out. Milo yells over Mateo until Mateo stops talking.
- Mateo goes quiet, picks up his ball, and walks away.
- **Discovery page:** as Mateo turns, Milo sees five squares above his head, and **two of them go dark**. Milo rubs his eyes. *(First meter in the book.)*
- **Home that evening:** Henry is reading on the couch and shouts "PLOT TWIST!" Milo asks what that is. Henry, still reading: "When the story suddenly goes a different way. It's the best part. You never know what happens next." Milo says nothing, but he files it away.
- **The experiments** (the high-IQ pages): Milo now sees meters on Mom, Dad, and Henry. Boris has nine. He tests: he says "Thanks, Dad" and a square fills. He grumbles at Henry and a square empties. He's figuring out the rules.

### Act 3: Change 2, **Stumble**: the swings (pp. 13–19)
- **Playground.** Milo wants tag. Nia wants to turn the swings into a "sky kingdom."
- The NO jumps out. Nia's meter drops. **This time Milo sees it happen**, and he freezes.
- He tries to fix it, badly: "FINE. We'll play your dumb kingdom." *(Another square drops; the stumble.)*
- He tries again, quieter: "...What's your idea?" Nia explains, a square returns, and they swing. It's okay, not great. *Squares come back slower than they go.*
- **His own meter:** he hops off the swing and looks into the puddle. Above his own reflection is a meter, and it's low too. Yelling emptied *his*.

### Act 4: Change 3, **Success**: the piano (pp. 20–28)
- **Home.** Mateo and Nia come over. Milo plays his song on the piano, his way.
- Nia slides onto the bench and starts playing along, right over him.
- **The NO rises** (drawn as a word bubble pushing up from his chest). Milo squeezes his eyes shut, holds it, and thinks: *Plot twist?* *(Refrain payoff: "This time, Milo caught it.")*
- "PLOT TWIST! It's a duet now!" Mateo drums on a box. Henry looks up from his book. Boris doesn't move.
- **All meters fill**, square by square, including Milo's.
- **Final reveal:** Nia leans over: "Your meter's full, you know." Milo: "You can SEE it?!" Nia just smiles. *(Was she always able to?)*
- **Last page:** Henry, on the couch, turns a page: "Plot twist." Milo grins.

### Refrains (the repeated lines that carry the theme)
1. *"The NO jumped out before Milo could catch it."* (Acts 1–3) → *"This time, Milo caught it."* (Act 4)
2. *"Squares come back slower than they go."* (Acts 2–3)
3. *"Plot twist!"* (Henry in Act 2 → Milo in Act 4 → Henry on the last page)

## 7. Visual style guide

### 7.1 Style description (the "style anchor"; never name a real artist in prompts)
> Loose, confident ink linework with varied line weight; delicate transparent watercolor washes; a mostly white background with only suggested scenery (a hoop, a swing, a piano edge); a soft, muted, warm palette; expressive, exaggerated cartoon faces; dynamic kid poses; a hand-drawn newspaper-comic feel; lots of negative space; calm and clear.

### 7.2 Page composition rules
- **Square** pages (target 8×8 in or 8.5×8.5 in **[OPEN]**), one frame per page, a single focal action.
- **Top ~20%:** keep clear behind characters' heads for meters.
- **Text zone:** a consistent white band (top or bottom **[OPEN]**), typeset in Canva, never generated.
- Characters stay at a consistent scale relative to each other: Nia is taller than Milo and Mateo; Henry is the tallest kid.
- Backgrounds stay sparse: 1–3 setting props per page. White wins.
- Emotional peaks (the discovery, the puddle, the duet) get **more white space**, not more detail.

### 7.3 Palette (proposal)
- Milo: **[OPEN]** hair color; primary-color striped shirt.
- Mateo: orange/teal (basketball tones).
- Nia: pinks, lavender, a gold accent on her clip.
- Henry: muted greens and browns.
- Boris: blue-grey with green eyes.
- Meter: golden yellow (§5.1). It's the warmest color in the book, so it always draws the eye.

### 7.4 Lettering
- A hand-lettered comic-style font for dialogue, and a clean, readable serif or rounded sans for narration. Final choice is made in Canva **[OPEN]**.
- "NO!" and "PLOT TWIST!" get special display lettering. The NO can be drawn as a jagged bubble that "jumps out."
